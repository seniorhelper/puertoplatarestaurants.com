Restaurant tipping and money in Puerto Plata

How a Dominican restaurant bill works

Read the bottom of the check before you reach for cash. A typical sit-down bill in Puerto Plata shows the food and drink subtotal, then two lines: ITBIS, the 18% sales tax set by Law 253-12, and servicio or ley, the 10% legal tip that Article 228 of the Labor Code requires hotels and restaurants to add for staff. Together they add about 28% to menu prices.

Some beach bars and casual places quote prices with everything included. The calculator’s checkbox lets you switch the extras off for those.

A worked example

Two people order RD$2,000 of food and drinks. The bill adds RD$200 of service (10%) and RD$360 of ITBIS (18%), for RD$2,560. Leaving an extra 10% in cash for the server adds RD$200, so you pay RD$2,760, or RD$1,380 each. At a rate of, say, 60 pesos to the dollar that is about US$46; type today’s real rate into the calculator for the exact figure.

Tipping on top

Because the 10% is pooled, and reports say it does not always reach servers in full, many visitors hand an extra 5–10% in cash directly to the person who looked after them. There is a long-running debate in Congress about making the 10% voluntary; as of our last check it remains on bills.

Paying

  • Cards are widely accepted in Playa Dorada, Cofresí resorts and better restaurants downtown.
  • Pesos are best at comedores, colmados, street stalls and for tips.
  • US dollars are sometimes accepted in tourist areas, at the restaurant’s own rate.
  • Use bank ATMs inside branches or malls in daylight.

Tipping questions

Is service included in Dominican restaurant bills?

Usually yes. Article 228 of the Dominican Labor Code requires restaurants and hotels to add a 10% legal tip (propina legal) for staff, and an 18% ITBIS tax applies on top of food and drink. Reports say a court ruling limits the 10% to dine-in orders.

Should I tip extra?

It is optional. Many diners leave an extra 5–10% in cash for the server when service is good, because the 10% charge is pooled and does not always reach the person who served you.

Do restaurants take US dollars?

Tourist-area restaurants sometimes do, at their own rate, which is often worse than a bank’s. Paying in pesos or by card is usually better value.
