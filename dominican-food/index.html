Dominican food guide

Dominican food in Puerto Plata: what to order

The dishes you will see on every menu, how they are eaten and where in town to try them.

Dominican cooking grew from three roots: Taíno ingredients such as yuca and corn, African cooking techniques and staples like plantain, and the Spanish pantry of rice, garlic, onions and pork. On the North Coast the ocean adds a fourth: fish, shellfish and the habit of eating them with a view.

A day of eating, Dominican style

Breakfast is mangú or eggs with salami and cheese, plus strong sweet coffee. Lunch, la comida, is the main meal between about noon and 2 p.m.: La Bandera at a comedor, or fish by the sea on weekends. Dinner is lighter at home, but restaurants serve full menus, and street stalls sell empanadas, chicharrón and frituras late into the evening.

Flavors to know

  • Sofrito (often called sazón): a base of onion, garlic, peppers, cilantro and tomato that starts most stews.
  • Ají caballero and ají gustoso: Dominican food is aromatic rather than fiery; hot sauce is optional.
  • Auyama: a squash that thickens stews and sancocho.
  • Chinola, lechosa, guineo: passion fruit, papaya and banana, the backbone of the juice stand.

Where to try it in Puerto Plata

For an everyday lunch, look for a busy comedor in the historic center or try Skina. For fish with tostones by the water, the Malecón is the place. For Dominican flavors in a dressier setting, the Victorian-house restaurants downtown mix local ingredients into Caribbean-fusion menus.

Twelve dishes and drinks to know

La Bandera Dominicana

The national lunch: white rice, stewed red beans (habichuelas guisadas) and braised chicken, beef or pork, often with salad and tostones. Every comedor serves it at midday.

Sancocho

A thick stew of several meats with yuca, ñame, auyama (pumpkin), plantain and corn. It is the Sunday and celebration dish; the most generous versions use seven meats.

Mangú con los tres golpes

Boiled green plantains mashed smooth and topped with vinegar-sautéed onions, served with fried salami, fried cheese and fried eggs. Breakfast of champions.

Pescado a la criolla

Whole fish braised in a sofrito of tomato, peppers, onion and cilantro. A coastal staple on the North Coast.

Tostones and maduros

Tostones are green plantain slices fried, smashed and fried again until crisp. Maduros are ripe plantains fried until sweet and caramelized.

Locrio

The Dominican cousin of paella: rice cooked with chicken, sausage or seafood and achiote for color.

Chicharrón

Crispy fried pork pieces sold by roadside vendors and at weekend stands, eaten with lime and yuca.

Empanadas and pastelitos

Fried turnovers filled with cheese, chicken or beef, sold at bakeries and school corners.

Pastel en hoja

Plantain or yuca dough filled with seasoned meat, wrapped in a leaf and boiled, a holiday favorite.

Habichuelas con dulce

A sweet, spiced cream of red beans with coconut milk, sweet potato and raisins, made around Lent and Easter.

Mamajuana

Rum and red wine steeped with honey, tree bark and roots. More a ritual than a cocktail.

Morir soñando

Orange juice whisked with milk and sugar over ice, a creamy favorite at juice stands.

Dominican food FAQ

What is mangú?

Mashed boiled green plantains, served with sautéed onions and usually with fried salami, cheese and eggs ("los tres golpes"). It is the classic Dominican breakfast and is served at comedores and cafés until about noon.

What is sancocho in the Dominican Republic?

A slow-cooked stew of several meats, root vegetables, plantain and corn, served with white rice and avocado. It is the celebration dish for Sundays, weddings and holidays.

Are there vegetarian options in Dominican food?

Traditional cooking is meat-centric, but rice and beans, tostones, maduros, yuca, salads and fresh fruit are everywhere. Ask whether beans are cooked with meat. International restaurants in Playa Dorada and the center offer more choice.

Does Puerto Plata have good coffee and cacao?

Coffee grows in the Cordillera Septentrional mountains behind the city, and cacao is grown in the region, so local coffee and chocolate are easy to find in cafés and shops.
